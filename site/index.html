A layout annealed on my own words

A split keyboard whose letters were rearranged to fit what I actually type, and a trainer to learn them.

Try the trainer Firmware source

Why move the letters at all

QWERTY was drawn for typewriter arms, not for hands. On a small split board every letter can sit where it costs the least to reach, so I had a program search for that arrangement using 1.7 million characters of my own writing. The search is called annealing; it is step 4 below. Here is what came out of it.

How the letters were chosen

Five steps. Every number comes from the scripts in the repository, so anyone can rerun them on their own writing.

  1. 1Measure what I type

    A layout should fit the words it will type, so the first job was to collect mine. I took every message I had typed to an AI assistant: the Claude Code history and the claude.ai export. Pasted text, code, web addresses and logs were thrown out, duplicates removed, and each message was tagged by language from its small common words. 1,677,299 characters remained.

    French was an eighth of that sample although it is the language of my work, so I weighted it up to a third of the mix.

  2. 2Price every key

    Some keys are cheap to reach and some are not. The eight home keys, the ones under the fingers at rest, cost 1. Every other key costs 1 plus its distance from the finger’s home key, times a penalty for the weaker fingers: 1.6 under a pinky, 1.2 under a ring finger, 1.3 on the inner columns the index has to stretch to. The far corners cost 3.2.

    The positions come from beekeeb’s own firmware file for the Toucan2: where each column sits, which finger owns it, which key is its home.

    The cost of each of the thirty core keys. Darker is dearer.

  3. 3Define the score

    Reach is not the whole story: two letters in a row with the same finger are slow even on cheap keys. So the score adds up eight habits, each measured as a share of my letter pairs or triples, times a weight I chose. Lower is better.

    The weights are a judgment, not a law: other weights give another board. An early run with no cap on finger load put 16% of all keystrokes on each ring finger, which is why the last term exists.

  4. 4Search by annealing

    There are 30! ways to place thirty letters, 2.65 × 1032, far too many to try. Simulated annealing searches such a space the way metal is cooled slowly to settle: start from a random board, swap two keys, score again. Better, keep it. Worse, keep it anyway with a probability that shrinks as the run cools, so the search can climb out of a dead end early and settles late.

    Below, seed 5’s run: 60,000 swaps, the score falling from 132 to 25.5. Early, one worsening swap in six is kept; by the end, none. Eight runs from eight random starts scored 480,000 boards in all.

  5. 5Pick, and say why

    Three of the eight runs finished within five thousandths of each other, which says the search had converged rather than landed by luck. Seed 1 is seed 5 mirrored, so I took the orientation with the vowels on the right hand, as most modern layouts do.

    Seed 0 scores 0.005 better, but it puts r on a home key where seed 5 puts n, and I type n a fifth more often.

Two halves, no dongle

The keyboard runs ZMK, open-source firmware. The two halves talk over Bluetooth, with no receiver between them and the computer.

A trainer that knows which keys lag

Speed times accuracy opens the next lesson

Every key gets a score: how close your speed and accuracy on it are to the target. The next lesson opens once all the new keys are green.

Chunks and weak pairs

Common letter groups (tion, ment, ing) and the pairs you type slowly come back more often, until they stop being slow.

The board mirrors the USB key presses

Plugged in by USB, the keyboard reports each real press and the board lights the key you hit. Your progress follows you between machines with a secret code, no account.
